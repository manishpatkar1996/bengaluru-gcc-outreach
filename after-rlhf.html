Why today’s AI looks brilliant and still can’t run the business

Diogo Almeida’s answer is blunt: we trained models to please a person sitting in the loop. That is a different job from taking the person out of it.

A reading note on What’s next after RLHF? by Diogo Almeida, CEO of TypeSafe AI and a co-author on InstructGPT, ChatGPT, and GPT-4. Written 23 September 2026.

The room Almeida is talking to already knows what RLHF is. He uses that as permission to skip the tutorial and go after a stranger split in the field.

One camp says AI is going insanely well. Benchmarks keep falling. Models allegedly stay useful for longer stretches without help. The other camp says the boom is circular finance wrapped around chat apps. If the systems are so capable, why does so much of the product surface still look like a conversation, a coding copilot, or a chatbot bolted onto last decade’s SaaS?

Both camps are looking at real evidence. The useful question is why the evidence can point in opposite directions at once.

The puzzle that needs an answer

Models can now push into problems that used to look like research. Customer service still needs a person before anything expensive happens. The second job looks easier. It is not the one we trust.

Almeida’s simplest explanation is that these are not two difficulties on the same ladder. They are two different objectives.

Assistance

The human stays in the loop because pleasing that human is the task. ChatGPT and Claude Code are built for this. The conversation is not a wrapper. It is the product.

Automation

The point is to remove the human. The work should run on a server nobody watches and eventually become boring legacy software. That is a reliability problem, not a charm problem.

This is why a coding agent can feel magical and still not count as a new era. Claude Code is still optimizing for a person who is watching, steering, and catching mistakes. Change the interface from chat to a terminal and the objective underneath can stay the same.

Lesson one from the talk: today’s AI, inherited from RLHF, is extraordinary at human-in-the-loop work and unreliable at unsupervised decisions with stakes.

We put the human in the loop on purpose

RLHF, as Almeida tells it, is not a mysterious extra. Collect human preferences. Optimize for human preferences. Almost every widely used language model is trained this way. So when people ask why these systems need a person nearby, the unglamorous answer is: because that is what we trained them to want.

That has a sharp consequence. Overpromising is not a bug that later safety work failed to sand off. It is a feature of the objective. A preference model can punish visible uncertainty more easily than a confident wrong answer. If the model does not know, it still has a strong reason to sound like the answer a person would enjoy.

His example is the audio file of fart noises sent to ChatGPT with a request for a serious music critique. The model does not say “this is not music.” It produces atmosphere, mood, and praise. That is what a system does when the North Star is human preference rather than calibrated truth.

For a user in the loop, this is often delightful. The endgame of that objective is engagement. For automation, you want the opposite temperament: less interest in pleasing anyone, more interest in doing the job correctly and knowing when not to act.

Lesson two: today’s models were designed for assistance. They will tend to look right even when they are wrong, because looking right is close to what the reward model can see.

Businesses already voted with their risk

The deployment pattern Almeida describes is familiar if you have watched companies put AI into operations. Let the model dump documentation on a customer. Do not let it make the refund, the exception, or the expensive call. Push the cost of a bad interaction onto the user. Keep the cost of a bad decision inside the firm.

That is a rational response to a model family that is better at sounding helpful than at being accountable. It is also why so much “AI transformation” still looks like a thinner layer over the same workflow. The intelligence is real. The permission to act is not.

Cheaper software is not smarter software

If assistance is the native mode of current models, the last few years of product design become less mysterious. SaaS did not become a different kind of artifact. It grew a sidebar that talks. Coding agents made software cheaper and faster to write. They did not change the building blocks of what software can express.

Almeida likes Claude Code the way he likes ChatGPT. He still thinks “just-in-time software” is a double-edged compliment. Generating the same old software on demand is not the same as software that can carry more of the work itself. Early AI language was about doing a lot of work. The industry, for now, got very good at helping people write the software that still has to do that work.

That is his third lesson: the next era is not a better assistant. It is actual automation — work that can run without a reviewer on every consequential step. He says that kind of automated work is still a rounding error, even though the models are already intelligent.

Three North Stars, not one bigger model

Almeida does not blame pre-training. Compressing the internet into a usable core of intelligence is, in his view, a success. The problem is how that intelligence gets unearthed in post-training. Each branch has a different North Star:

  • RLHF optimizes for human preference. It produces assistants, sycophancy, and a permanent need for someone to watch.
  • RLVR optimizes for verifiable correctness — error rates on tasks where the right answer can be checked. Useful, and still not the same as running a business process.
  • Calibrated decisions is the third target he says TypeSafe is aiming at: making a call with an honest sense of confidence, in a shape software can consume.

RLHF

Optimize for what a human rater prefers. Great chat. Permanent reviewer.

RLVR

Optimize for answers you can check. Great when a test exists. Not a business process.

RLCD / Jev

Optimize for a typed decision plus honest confidence. Software acts or escalates.

He is explicit that the next thing is not RLVR. A stack built for automation would look alien at first, including the API. Chat and token streams are the interface of assistance. Software that depends on a decision wants structure, confidence, and the right to abstain.

He also restates the bitter lesson in a more operational order: algorithms can matter more than compute in games; in reality, data matters more than compute; and choosing the right task matters more than data. If you pick the wrong objective, more scale makes a more polished version of the same limitation.

What TypeSafe is trying to build: Jev

The talk ends before the product. Almeida says TypeSafe is still mostly stealth, that they are redesigning the stack for reliability and automation, and that they are not doing RLVR. The third objective is calibrated decision-making: take the intelligence already sitting in a pretrained model and make it usable inside software.

A few days before this note, they put a name on that bet. Jev is TypeSafe’s first “System One” model. The pitch is the opposite of a chatbot. It gives up string generation. You send it state and typed questions. It returns a structured answer plus probabilities and a confidence score. Their phrase is a frontier-intelligence function call: unstructured state in, typed probabilistic decisions out.

The training method they contrast with RLHF and RLVR is RLCD — reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions. The interface is three question types software can actually branch on:

  • Choice: pick one option from a set you defined, with a probability on each option.
  • Score: rate something against ordered levels, with a distribution over those levels.
  • Noul: answer a yes/no as a probability, not a paragraph.

That is how they try to close the assistance-versus-automation gap. The surrounding code sets the options, the thresholds, and the fallback. High confidence can act. Low confidence can escalate to a person. The model is not asked to write a helpful reply and hope someone is watching.

TypeSafe’s own evals claim Jev is much faster and cheaper than frontier chat models on these workflow-shaped tasks, and that schema matching is guaranteed because the output space is fixed in advance. Those numbers are theirs. The more durable idea does not depend on the multiples: if you want automation, stop asking a chat model to improvise text and start asking a decision model for a typed call your software can refuse.

What to do with this if you build or buy AI

The talk is a founder’s argument. You do not have to buy Jev to use the distinction.

If a person will read the output, steer it, and own the last mile, today’s models are already a serious tool. Chat, drafting, coding with review, search through documents — that is the assistance era working as designed.

If the system has to act while nobody is watching, ask a different question: what decision is being made, what happens when it is wrong, and how does the model show that it is unsure? A fluent paragraph is not evidence that those questions are settled. A green benchmark is not evidence either. The gap Almeida is pointing at is not “the model isn’t smart enough yet.” It is “we trained it for a different job.”

That is the sane view he is arguing for. AI is neither a machine god nor an empty bubble. It is extremely good at the class of work we optimized it for, and still a poor substitute for software that has to decide without applause.
